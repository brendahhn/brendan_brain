## Health

### [CONC] Your morning cup of tea is fine — the thing to watch is the *extract*, not the *drink*
<sub>source: [`from-health-robot.md`](queue/inbox/from-health-robot.md)</sub>

This is a lighter idea-queue answer ("what's the best morning hot tea?"), **not** one of your 07-23 batch tasks — plain consumer-nutrition science, no personal data.

- **[B]** **A brewed cup and an extract capsule are different animals — the difference is dose.** High-dose green-tea *extract* (~≥800 mg EGCG/day per EFSA) can inhibit drug-metabolizing liver enzymes and carries an idiosyncratic liver-injury signal; a **brewed cup sits far below that** and does neither. Rule: enjoy the drink freely; treat a green-tea *extract supplement* as a "run it past your doctor" item, especially on regular meds.
- **[A]** **The one real catch is iron.** Tea tannins cut non-heme iron absorption from a meal sharply (~79–94% in the classic study). Easy fix: **don't drink tea *with* an iron-rich meal or an iron supplement — leave about an hour.** Between meals it's a non-issue.
- **[B]** **"Tea causes kidney stones" is overblown** — tea's oxalate is largely non-absorbable, and controlled data show daily tea didn't raise urinary oxalate even in stone-formers. **Rooibos** is essentially oxalate-free, and **[C]** the cleanest caffeine-free option overall (no caffeine, low tannin so it doesn't block iron, no known drug interactions).
- **[B]** **Chamomile caveat:** it's in the ragweed/mugwort (Asteraceae) family and can cross-react with **weed-pollen allergies** in a small number of people. Worth knowing if that's you.
- **[B]** Green tea has a small genuine anti-inflammatory signal in ulcerative colitis — but that was at *extract* doses, so the cup is a **mild bonus, not a treatment.**
