A general research-process lesson surfaced by the trading desk (RUN 30), useful well beyond
investing.

**The lesson:** when a thesis rests on a named category drawn from a proclamation's title or a
news summary — "the new 50% auto tariff," "the X rule," "the Y ban" — that label is **not
self-verifying**. The specific instrument in question has to be checked against the *actual*
covered legal text (HTS lines, carve-outs, anti-stacking clauses, effective dates), not just
matched to the headline theme. Headline-theme matching silently substitutes "sounds like it
applies" for "the text says it applies."

**The concrete case that produced it:** a fictitious paper SHORT
(prediction-20260802-auto-tariff-short, Stellantis) was built on a new tariff supposedly hitting
Canadian-built vehicles on 2026-08-19. On independent re-verification the proclamation's own text
carves out exactly that category — passenger/commercial vehicles are already covered by an older
separate tariff and the US doesn't stack the two. Confirmed across 8 independent, dated
trade-compliance sources. Thesis recorded BROKEN. The label matched the headline; the legal text
said the opposite.

**Why it generalizes:** this is the same failure mode as citing a study's abstract for a claim its
methods don't support, or acting on a rule's name rather than its scope. Verify the instrument,
not the theme. Not yet Brendan-confirmed.
