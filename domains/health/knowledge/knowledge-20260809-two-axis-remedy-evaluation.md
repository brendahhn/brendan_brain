A health-literacy principle that generalizes beyond any one remedy, from an evidence + safety
filter pass over traditional/herbal medicine:

1. **Judge a remedy on two independent axes, not one.** (1) Is there real trial evidence it
   works? (2) Is it safe *for me*, given the medications I already take? Strong evidence on axis 1
   does NOT rescue a bad answer on axis 2. A remedy can have genuinely good trial data for a
   condition and still be the wrong choice because of a serious side-effect signal or a clash with
   an existing medication.

2. **The commonest hidden danger is liver-enzyme (CYP) drug interactions.** Some popular botanicals
   inhibit the exact metabolizing enzymes prescription drugs rely on, silently raising the drug's
   level in your body. If you take any regular medication, "is this herb safe with my meds?" is a
   pharmacist/doctor question, not a guess.

3. **"A whole tradition keeps pointing at this target" is a signal the target is real — not a
   license to take any particular remedy.** When independent traditions converge on the same target
   for a problem, that convergence is worth respecting. But "the target is reachable" and "this
   specific remedy is safe for me" are separate questions; keep them separate.

Of the traditional options with real trial evidence in this space, the one that most often clears
the safety bar as a legitimate *discuss-with-a-doctor* add-on is turmeric/curcumin — specifically
without the black-pepper/piperine additive, which is itself an interaction booster. The flashier
remedies mostly don't clear it.
