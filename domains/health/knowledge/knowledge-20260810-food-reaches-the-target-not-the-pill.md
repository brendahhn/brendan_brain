A follow-on to the two-axis remedy filter (does it work / is it safe for me): when a
strong-but-unsafe remedy proves that some target in the body is genuinely *reachable*, the next
question is not "how do I get that remedy safely" — it's **"what ordinary food or habit reaches
the same target?"** The answer is often a vegetable you already know you should eat, at zero cost
and near-zero risk.

1. **Reachability is the transferable finding, not the remedy.** A risky supplement or botanical
   is useful mainly as proof that a pathway responds to diet/intake at all. Once that's
   established, look for the whole-food lever before reaching for a product.

2. **Concrete instance:** cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, kale,
   cauliflower) plus a well-fed gut microbiome (fiber, fermented foods, adequate protein) feed a
   real diet→immune→gut-lining signaling system. That's a good, low-cost reason to keep these as a
   standing habit. Honest caveat: the strongest evidence is still lab/animal work, so treat it as
   a sensible habit, not a cure.

3. **The same-compound-different-form trap.** The concentrated *supplement* of a vegetable's active
   compound (here, "DIM"/I3C capsules marketed for gut/hormone health) can behave completely
   differently from the food — this one nudges liver enzymes in a way that can lower the blood
   level of medications. The vegetable's dose is negligible; the pill is a genuine interaction
   risk. Eat the food; skip the concentrated capsule if you take any regular medication (a
   pharmacist question, not a guess).

4. **"Antioxidant vegetable compounds" are not interchangeable.** Two compounds from the very same
   vegetable can hit different receptors and even work against each other — another argument for
   eating the whole food (you get the balanced mix) rather than isolating one compound into a
   high-dose pill.
